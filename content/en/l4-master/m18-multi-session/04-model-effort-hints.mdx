## Objectives & prerequisites

By the end of this lesson you can:

- Explain what `model_hint` and `effort_hint` are for, and why they are advice to the claiming session rather than a gate on it.
- Map a piece of work to a hint pair using the kind of judgement it needs, not its size.
- Recognise the moment a hint is wrong — you are inventing a decision — and escalate instead of guessing.
- Connect a plan's hint to the mechanisms that actually set a model or effort level: `--model`, `/effort`, `modelSettings`, and a subagent's `model` and `effort` frontmatter.

**Prerequisites.** Lessons 01–03 of this module, and Level 2's models-and-effort module, which covers the model family and the effort levels themselves.

<WhenNotToUse>
  Do not put hints on a plan set where every plan is the same kind of work — six identical
  translation plans do not need six copies of the same advice, and a field that always reads the
  same trains people to ignore it. Do not use hints as a budget control: they are per-plan advice,
  while spend is governed by usage limits and the choices a session actually makes. And do not
  enforce them. The moment a hint becomes a rule, a session that needs to escalate has to break the
  rule to do the right thing, which is a worse failure than a plan being worked on a stronger model
  than someone predicted.
</WhenNotToUse>

## Concept

### Two fields, one purpose

`model_hint` and `effort_hint` sit in a plan's frontmatter alongside `estimate`. They exist because the session that _writes_ a plan usually knows more about the shape of the work than the session that _claims_ it, and that knowledge would otherwise be lost:

<CodeBlock title="Hint fields in a plan's frontmatter">

```yaml
model_hint: opus # haiku | sonnet | opus | fable
effort_hint: high # low | medium | high | xhigh | max
estimate: L # S (≤0.5 day) | M (≤1 day) | L (≤2 days)
```

</CodeBlock>

They are printed next to every claimable plan, so choosing what to work on and choosing what to work on it _with_ is one decision rather than two:

### Assign by the judgement required

The useful axis is not how long the work takes but how much judgement it needs — specifically, how likely the session is to face a decision the plan does not already make for it.

| Hint                     | Meaning                                                                 | Typical work                                                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `haiku` / `low`          | Mechanical and fully specified: the answer is already written somewhere | Sources sweeps, fixtures, glue code, docs kept in sync                 |
| `sonnet` / `medium`      | Normal feature work with tests                                          | Infrastructure, CI wiring, deploy scripts, translation, UI components  |
| `opus` / `high`          | Judgement-heavy content and interface work                              | Lesson authoring, MDX components, interaction design                   |
| `fable` / `xhigh`, `max` | Architecture and system-level design judgement                          | A design canvas, a curriculum map, cross-cutting composition decisions |

The official effort guidance lines up with the right-hand column: `low` is for "short, scoped, latency-sensitive tasks that are not intelligence-sensitive"; `medium` for "cost-sensitive work that can trade off some intelligence"; `high` "balances token usage and intelligence" and is the default on most models; `xhigh` buys "deeper reasoning at higher token spend"; and of `max` the docs say it "can improve performance on demanding tasks but may show diminishing returns and is prone to overthinking. Test before adopting broadly."

That last warning is the one people ignore. `max` on mechanical work is not merely wasteful — it is worse, because a model given room to reason about a task with nothing to reason about will find something.

A healthy plan set therefore has a lopsided distribution, weighted towards the middle:

Here is the worked example prompt for this lesson — a question worth asking about your own plan set:

```text
Read every plan file's frontmatter and group the plans by model_hint and
effort_hint. Which plans carry a hint that does not match the kind of work
their Goal describes?
```

### A hint is not a gate

Nothing checks that the claiming session honours the hint, and that is the design. From the plan contract: "The hint is advice to the claiming session, not a gate: if you find yourself inventing a decision, escalate."

That sentence names the trigger precisely. Not "if the work feels hard" — **if you find yourself inventing a decision**. A plan that forces a guess about product, curriculum or architecture is a plan whose hint was too low, or whose Context was too thin. The response is to write the question into `open_questions` and the Handoff notes, take the most conservative reading, and if the whole plan is mis-hinted, say so, so the next session does not repeat the discovery.

### Where the hint actually lands

The plan file records intent; something else has to act on it. In practice:

- **Starting the session.** `claude --model opus` picks the model; the alias resolves to the latest model of that family. Effort comes from `--effort`, from `/effort` in the session (with no argument it opens a slider), from the slider inside `/model`, or from `CLAUDE_CODE_EFFORT_LEVEL`.
- **Per model, persistently.** `modelSettings` in your settings file sets an `effortLevel` per model, so each model keeps its own level.
- **Per subagent.** A subagent's frontmatter takes `model` (`sonnet`, `opus`, `haiku`, `fable`, a full model id, or `inherit`) and `effort` (`low` … `max`, overriding the session level). `CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_MODEL` sets a default for subagents that are not assigned a model another way; per-invocation models and the definition's own `model` field take precedence over it.

So a plan hinted `opus`/`high` typically becomes one session started on Opus at high effort, which may still delegate its mechanical sub-tasks to a `haiku` subagent. The hint describes the plan's hardest decision, not every action taken while executing it.

<Callout>
  Hints age. When a plan sits in `todo` for a month while the project's conventions solidify around
  it, work that once needed judgement may have become mechanical — the earlier plans wrote down the
  answers. Re-read the hint against the plan's Context before you trust it.
</Callout>

## Anti-patterns

**Hinting by size.** A two-day plan of mechanical edits does not need a stronger model than a one-hour architectural decision. _Fix:_ hint on judgement; `estimate` already carries size.

**`max` everywhere.** The docs warn it is "prone to overthinking", and it is the most expensive way to get a worse answer on a simple task. _Fix:_ reserve the top of the scale for work you have tested it on.

**Treating the hint as permission.** A session that refuses to escalate because "the plan says sonnet" will guess instead — and an invented decision is far more expensive than a stronger model. _Fix:_ escalate the moment you are inventing a decision, and record why in Handoff notes.

**Silent escalation.** Quietly working an `opus` plan on `fable` and saying nothing leaves the next session with a hint that mis-describes the work. _Fix:_ note the mismatch in the Handoff notes so the hint can be corrected.

**A flat distribution.** If every plan says `sonnet`/`medium`, nobody consulted the field when writing it. _Fix:_ either assign it deliberately or drop it; a field nobody reads is worse than no field.
